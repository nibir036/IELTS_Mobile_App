1. Success & Ambition

What it means to be successful and the role of ambition.

Q1Opinion
How do you define success?

I think success is deeply personal and far broader than wealth or status. To me, genuine success means finding fulfilment in what you do, maintaining good relationships, and feeling that you are growing and contributing something meaningful. While financial security matters, defining success purely by money seems rather hollow. So I would say true success is a sense of contentment and purpose, which looks different for every individual.

Vocabulary: hollow · contentment · purpose

Q2Comparison
Is success more about hard work or luck?

I believe it is a combination of both, though hard work is the factor within our control. Undeniably, luck plays a role, such as being born into favourable circumstances or being in the right place at the right time. However, hard work, persistence and seizing opportunities are what allow people to capitalise on that luck. So while luck can open doors, it is usually diligence and determination that sustain lasting success.

Vocabulary: diligence · determination · sustain

Q3Cause & Effect
Why do some people become more ambitious than others?

Ambition is shaped by a mix of factors. Upbringing plays a big role; children encouraged to aim high often internalise that drive. Personal experiences, including hardship, can also fuel a determination to succeed. Individual personality and the influence of role models matter too. Additionally, some are motivated by a fear of failure or a desire for recognition. So ambition usually stems from a blend of nurture, personal circumstance and innate temperament.

Vocabulary: nurture · circumstance · temperament

Q4Social Impact
Is ambition always a good thing?

Ambition is largely positive, as it drives progress, innovation and personal achievement. However, it has a darker side when taken to extremes. Unchecked ambition can lead people to become ruthless, neglecting ethics, relationships and their own wellbeing in the relentless pursuit of their goals. It can breed dissatisfaction too. So while healthy ambition is a wonderful motivator, it becomes harmful when it turns into an obsessive or ruthless drive that overrides everything else.

Vocabulary: obsessive · ruthless · overrides

Q5Future
Do you think the definition of success is changing?

Yes, I believe it is evolving, particularly among younger generations. The traditional markers of success, like a high salary and status, are increasingly being questioned. Many now place greater value on work-life balance, wellbeing, purpose and experiences over pure material wealth. There is a growing recognition that a fulfilling life matters more than a merely impressive one. So the definition of success seems to be shifting towards something more holistic and meaningful than materialistic.

Vocabulary: holistic · meaningful · materialistic

Q6Problem & Solution
How can society support people to achieve their goals?

Society can do much to help. Ensuring equal access to quality education and opportunities gives everyone a fairer chance, regardless of background. Providing mentorship, resources and support for entrepreneurs and innovators helps too. Creating a culture that values effort and does not stigmatise failure encourages people to take risks. Essentially, by removing barriers and fostering an enabling environment, society can help far more people fulfil their potential.

Vocabulary: enabling · fulfil · potential

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of being highly ambitious?

High ambition drives people to achieve great things, fuels motivation and resilience, and can lead to considerable success and personal growth. It gives life direction and purpose. The disadvantages, however, include the risk of chronic stress, burnout and neglecting personal relationships and health in the single-minded pursuit of goals. It can also breed perpetual dissatisfaction. So ambition is a powerful asset, provided it is balanced and does not become all-consuming.

Vocabulary: asset · balanced · consuming

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say money is the best measure of success. Do you agree?

I strongly disagree with this narrow view. While money is a tangible and convenient measure, and financial security certainly matters, equating it with success ignores far more important dimensions of a good life. Many wealthy people are deeply unhappy, while many people of modest means live rich, fulfilling lives. Reducing success to a bank balance seems reductive and misguided. True success, in my view, is measured by fulfilment, relationships and character, not currency.

Vocabulary: reductive · misguided · character

2. Happiness & Wellbeing

What makes people happy and the pursuit of a good life.

Q1Opinion
What do you think makes people truly happy?

In my view, lasting happiness comes primarily from within and from our connections, rather than from external possessions. Strong, loving relationships are consistently shown to be the greatest source of happiness. Beyond that, having a sense of purpose, good health, and the freedom to pursue what we find meaningful all contribute enormously. So I believe true happiness is rooted in connection, purpose and gratitude, not in material wealth.

Vocabulary: connection · purpose · gratitude

Q2Comparison
Are people happier now than in the past?

This is a fascinating and debated question. In material terms, we are far better off than previous generations, with more comfort, health and opportunity. Yet, curiously, rates of anxiety and depression seem to be rising, especially among the young. It appears that material progress has not straightforwardly translated into greater happiness. So while we have gained much, I am not convinced we are fundamentally happier, which suggests happiness depends on more than mere prosperity and comfort.

Vocabulary: prosperity · comfort · fundamentally

Q3Cause & Effect
Why do you think some wealthy people are unhappy?

This reflects the fact that money, beyond meeting basic needs, has diminishing returns on happiness. Wealthy people may still suffer from poor relationships, a lack of purpose, or the isolation and pressure that wealth can bring. There is also the phenomenon where people constantly adapt and want more, never feeling satisfied. So it demonstrates that happiness is not something money can simply buy; it depends on deeper, non-material fulfilment.

Vocabulary: buy · material · fulfilment

Q4Social Impact
How does a person's environment affect their happiness?

Our environment has a profound influence on our wellbeing. A supportive community, safe and pleasant surroundings, and access to nature and green space all contribute significantly to happiness. Conversely, a stressful, isolating or polluted environment can seriously undermine it. The people around us, our relationships and social connections, are perhaps the most important environmental factor of all. So happiness is not purely internal; it is profoundly shaped by our surroundings and relationships.

Vocabulary: shaped · surroundings · relationships

Q5Future
Do you think people will be happier in the future?

I am genuinely uncertain. On one hand, advances in medicine, technology and living standards could improve wellbeing. There is also growing awareness of mental health and the importance of a balanced life. On the other hand, rising pressures, social media comparison and uncertainty could work against happiness. So I think future happiness will depend less on material progress and more on whether we learn to prioritise the things that genuinely nourish human wellbeing.

Vocabulary: prioritise · nourish · wellbeing

Q6Problem & Solution
What can societies do to improve people's wellbeing?

Societies can take meaningful steps. Prioritising mental health services and reducing the stigma around them is crucial. Fostering community, reducing inequality, and ensuring people have access to nature, leisure and a healthy work-life balance all help. Some argue governments should measure national wellbeing, not just economic output. Essentially, by valuing human flourishing alongside economic growth, societies can create conditions in which more people can genuinely thrive and feel content.

Vocabulary: flourishing · thrive · content

Q7Adv & Disadv
Is the constant pursuit of happiness a good thing?

This is quite paradoxical. Striving for a good, meaningful life is clearly positive. However, obsessively pursuing happiness as a goal in itself can be counterproductive; it can create pressure and dissatisfaction when we inevitably experience negative emotions, which are a normal part of life. Chasing constant positivity is unrealistic. So I would argue that accepting the full range of human emotions and finding meaning is healthier than a relentless, anxious pursuit of fleeting pleasure.

Vocabulary: pursuit · fleeting · pleasure

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say happiness is a choice. Do you agree?

I partly agree, but with important reservations. There is real truth that our attitude, gratitude and how we respond to circumstances significantly influence our happiness, so mindset matters. However, saying happiness is simply a choice can be dismissive of those facing genuine hardship, mental illness or difficult circumstances beyond their control. So while we have some agency over our happiness, I think it is oversimplified to call it purely a choice, ignoring the structural and biological factors involved.

Vocabulary: oversimplified · structural · biological

3. Money & Wealth

Attitudes to money, wealth distribution, and materialism.

Q1Opinion
How important is money in life?

Money is undeniably important, but its importance has limits. It is essential for meeting our basic needs, providing security, and giving us freedom and opportunities, so I would never dismiss it. However, beyond a certain point of comfort, its ability to improve our happiness diminishes sharply. Health, relationships and purpose matter far more. So I see money as a vital tool and enabler, but a poor ultimate goal in itself.

Vocabulary: tool · enabler · goal

Q2Comparison
Is it better to save money or spend it?

A healthy balance between the two is ideal. Saving is prudent, providing security, peace of mind and the means to achieve future goals or weather emergencies. However, being excessively frugal can mean missing out on valuable experiences and enjoyment in the present. Spending wisely, particularly on experiences and things that genuinely enrich life, is worthwhile. So I favour disciplined saving combined with mindful spending, avoiding both reckless extravagance and joyless hoarding.

Vocabulary: extravagance · hoarding · prudent

Q3Cause & Effect
What effect does the pursuit of wealth have on people?

The pursuit of wealth has mixed effects. Positively, it can motivate hard work, innovation and achievement, driving both personal and economic progress. However, an excessive obsession with wealth can be corrosive, leading people to become greedy, materialistic, and to neglect relationships, ethics and their own wellbeing. It can foster perpetual dissatisfaction too. So while a healthy desire to prosper is fine, an all-consuming fixation on money can genuinely distort a person's values.

Vocabulary: fixation · distort · values

Q4Social Impact
How does the gap between rich and poor affect society?

A large wealth gap has damaging effects on society. Extreme inequality tends to breed social tension, resentment and instability, and can undermine social mobility, trapping people in poverty. It is often associated with higher crime and poorer public health. It can also erode social cohesion and trust in institutions. So while some inequality is perhaps inevitable, a vast and growing gap is genuinely corrosive, weakening the social fabric and threatening stability.

Vocabulary: corrosive · fabric · stability

Q5Future
Do you think the gap between rich and poor will grow in the future?

I am concerned that it might, without deliberate intervention. Trends like automation, which may concentrate wealth among those who own the technology, and globalisation could widen the gap further. However, there is also growing awareness of inequality and political pressure to address it through progressive taxation and social policy. So the outcome is not predetermined; whether the gap widens or narrows will depend on the political choices societies make to redistribute opportunity.

Vocabulary: political · choices · redistribute

Q6Problem & Solution
What can be done to reduce poverty?

Reducing poverty requires a comprehensive approach. Access to quality education and healthcare is fundamental, as it enables social mobility. Creating decent, well-paid jobs and ensuring fair wages tackles the root causes. Social safety nets, like unemployment support, protect the most vulnerable. Progressive taxation can fund these efforts. Ultimately, empowering people with opportunities and skills, rather than mere handouts, offers the most sustainable path out of poverty.

Vocabulary: skills · sustainable · poverty

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of a cashless society?

A cashless society offers convenience, speed and enhanced security against theft, and it makes transactions easier to track, which can reduce crime and tax evasion. However, the disadvantages are significant: it excludes those without bank access or digital skills, particularly the elderly and poor, raising concerns about exclusion. It also enables greater surveillance of our spending and leaves us vulnerable to system failures. So it offers efficiency but at some cost to privacy and inclusion.

Vocabulary: exclusion · privacy · inclusion

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say money cannot buy happiness. Do you agree?

I largely agree, though with a nuance. It is certainly true that beyond meeting our basic needs, more money does not reliably increase happiness, and the richest people are not necessarily the happiest. Lasting happiness comes from relationships and purpose. However, I would add that a lack of money, and the poverty and stress it brings, can definitely cause unhappiness. So money cannot buy happiness, but financial security can certainly remove a major source of misery and anxiety.

Vocabulary: security · misery · anxiety

4. Equality & Social Justice

Fairness, discrimination, and equal opportunity in society.

Q1Opinion
Do you think society treats everyone equally?

Unfortunately, I do not think any society treats everyone truly equally, though many have made real progress. Despite laws promoting equality, discrimination based on factors like gender, ethnicity, class or background still persists, often subtly. People's opportunities are still heavily influenced by the circumstances of their birth. So while the ideal of equality is widely embraced, in practice significant disparities and injustices remain that we must continue to address.

Vocabulary: disparities · injustices · address

Q2Comparison
Has society become more or less equal over time?

In many respects, society has become considerably more equal. Enormous progress has been made in areas like women's rights, racial equality and the rights of minorities compared to the past. Legal discrimination has been dismantled in many places. However, economic inequality has actually widened in some countries. So the picture is mixed; we have made great strides in social and legal equality, while economic disparities remain a persistent challenge.

Vocabulary: economic · disparities · challenge

Q3Cause & Effect
What are the main causes of inequality in society?

Inequality stems from a complex web of factors. Unequal access to quality education and healthcare is a major driver, as it limits opportunity from birth. Discrimination based on gender, race or class also plays a role. Economic systems that concentrate wealth, along with the circumstances one is born into, perpetuate the cycle. So inequality is rarely due to a single cause; it results from structural, historical and social factors that reinforce one another.

Vocabulary: structural · historical · reinforce

Q4Social Impact
Why is equal opportunity important?

Equal opportunity is fundamental to a just and thriving society. It ensures that people can succeed based on their talent and effort, rather than being held back by circumstances of birth they cannot control. This is not only fair but also benefits society by allowing everyone's potential to be realised. Without it, talent is wasted and resentment grows. So equal opportunity is essential for both social justice and a society's overall prosperity and cohesion.

Vocabulary: justice · prosperity · cohesion

Q5Future
Do you think full equality is achievable?

I think complete equality of outcome is probably unrealistic, given natural differences in talent, effort and luck. However, equality of opportunity, where everyone has a genuinely fair chance to succeed, is a worthy and achievable goal to strive towards. We may never reach a perfectly equal society, but we can continually reduce unfair barriers. So while perfect equality may be an ideal rather than a destination, meaningful progress towards fairness is absolutely attainable.

Vocabulary: ideal · fairness · attainable

Q6Problem & Solution
How can governments promote greater equality?

Governments have powerful tools. Ensuring universal access to quality education and healthcare is perhaps the most important, as it levels the playing field. Progressive taxation and social welfare programmes can reduce economic disparities. Strong anti-discrimination laws protect marginalised groups. Investing in disadvantaged communities helps break cycles of poverty. Essentially, by actively removing barriers and redistributing opportunity, governments can foster a more equitable and inclusive society.

Vocabulary: redistributing · equitable · inclusive

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of policies that favour disadvantaged groups?

Such policies, like affirmative action, can help redress historical injustices and give disadvantaged groups a genuine chance, promoting diversity and breaking cycles of exclusion. The disadvantages are that they can be seen as unfair to others and may sometimes prioritise group identity over individual merit, occasionally breeding resentment. So while I believe such measures can be a necessary corrective for deep inequality, they must be carefully designed to be fair and temporary.

Vocabulary: corrective · inequality · fair

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say a completely equal society is neither possible nor desirable. Do you agree?

I partly agree. A society with perfectly equal outcomes is probably neither achievable nor, arguably, desirable, since some inequality reflecting effort and talent can incentivise productivity and reward hard work. However, I strongly believe that gross inequality is both unjust and harmful. So while I agree that absolute equality is unrealistic, this should not be used as an excuse for complacency; striving for genuine fairness and opportunity remains an essential imperative.

Vocabulary: complacency · fairness · imperative

5. Freedom & Individual Rights

Personal freedom, its limits, and the balance with collective needs.

Q1Opinion
How important is personal freedom?

I regard personal freedom as one of the most precious human values. The ability to make our own choices, express ourselves and live according to our own beliefs is fundamental to human dignity and fulfilment. Without freedom, people cannot truly flourish. However, I also believe freedom is not absolute; it must be balanced with responsibility to others. So while freedom is indispensable, it works best within a framework that also respects the collective good.

Vocabulary: indispensable · respects · collective

Q2Comparison
Which is more important, individual freedom or the safety of society?

This is a classic tension, and I believe the two must be carefully balanced rather than one always trumping the other. Individual freedom is precious, but a society cannot function if that freedom endangers others, so some limits are necessary for collective safety. The danger is when safety is used to justify excessive restrictions on liberty. So neither is absolutely paramount; the challenge is finding a proportionate balance that preserves both liberty and security.

Vocabulary: proportionate · preserves · security

Q3Cause & Effect
What happens when a society has too little freedom?

A society with too little freedom tends to suffer greatly. Suppressing free expression and choice stifles creativity, innovation and progress, as people cannot challenge ideas or think independently. It breeds fear, conformity and resentment, and often enables abuses of power to go unchecked. Human potential is squandered. So excessive restriction of freedom typically leads to stagnation, oppression and a profound loss of human dignity and vitality.

Vocabulary: stagnation · oppression · dignity

Q4Social Impact
Should there be limits on freedom of speech?

This is a delicate balance. I am a strong advocate of free speech as essential to a healthy democracy, allowing ideas to be debated openly. However, I believe some reasonable limits are justified, such as prohibiting incitement to violence or hatred that directly harms others. The difficulty lies in where to draw the line without enabling censorship. So while free speech should be robustly protected, I accept there is a legitimate case for narrow, carefully defined restrictions.

Vocabulary: narrow · defined · restrictions

Q5Future
Do you think people will have more or less freedom in the future?

I have mixed feelings about this. In some ways, technology empowers people and gives them a greater voice. However, the same technology enables unprecedented surveillance by governments and corporations, which could erode privacy and freedom. The tension between security, technology and liberty will be a defining issue. So the future of freedom is genuinely uncertain, and I believe it will require constant vigilance to safeguard our liberties against creeping encroachment.

Vocabulary: vigilance · safeguard · encroachment

Q6Problem & Solution
How can a balance be struck between freedom and security?

Striking this balance requires careful, transparent judgement. Any restriction on freedom in the name of security should be proportionate, necessary, and subject to independent oversight and accountability, rather than granted unchecked. Robust legal frameworks and an independent judiciary are essential safeguards. Public debate about these trade-offs is also healthy. Essentially, the key is ensuring that security measures are accountable and do not quietly erode the very liberties they claim to protect.

Vocabulary: accountable · erode · liberties

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of a society with very few rules?

A society with few rules offers maximum individual freedom, autonomy and space for creativity and self-expression, which can be liberating and dynamic. However, the disadvantages are considerable: without sufficient rules, there is a risk of chaos, exploitation of the weak by the strong, and a breakdown of order and safety. Rules protect people and enable cooperation. So while excessive rules are stifling, too few can lead to disorder; the ideal is a balanced, reasonable framework.

Vocabulary: disorder · balanced · reasonable

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say people should be free to do whatever they want. Do you agree?

I disagree with this as an absolute principle. While I deeply value personal freedom, unlimited freedom is unworkable, because one person's unrestrained actions can easily harm others. My freedom to act ends where it infringes on someone else's rights or safety. A functioning society requires that we balance our own liberty with responsibility towards others. So freedom must operate within reasonable limits; it is about liberty with accountability, not unchecked licence.

Vocabulary: reasonable · accountability · licence

6. Crime & Punishment

The causes of crime and how societies should respond to it.

Q1Opinion
What do you think are the main causes of crime?

I believe crime usually stems from a combination of social and personal factors rather than pure wickedness. Poverty, inequality, lack of education and unemployment often create conditions in which crime flourishes, as people feel they have few legitimate options. Poor upbringing, addiction and social exclusion also play a role. So while individuals bear responsibility, I think addressing the underlying social roots of crime is essential to genuinely reducing it.

Vocabulary: underlying · roots · reducing

Q2Comparison
Is it better to prevent crime or to punish it?

I firmly believe prevention is far better than punishment. Addressing the root causes of crime, through education, opportunity and social support, stops it before it happens, sparing potential victims and saving society enormous costs. Punishment, while necessary, is essentially reactive; it deals with the damage after it is done. So while a justice system is essential, I would argue that investing in prevention is a more humane and effective long-term strategy.

Vocabulary: prevention · humane · effective

Q3Cause & Effect
What effect does prison have on criminals?

The effects vary and are much debated. Ideally, prison should deter crime, protect society and rehabilitate offenders. However, in reality, prisons often fail to rehabilitate; they can even become schools for crime, where offenders learn worse behaviour, and the stigma makes reintegration difficult. High reoffending rates suggest this. So while prison serves important functions, I think an overreliance on incarceration without genuine rehabilitation can be counterproductive, perpetuating a cycle of crime.

Vocabulary: rehabilitation · counterproductive · cycle

Q4Social Impact
Should the purpose of prison be punishment or rehabilitation?

I believe the primary purpose should be rehabilitation, alongside protecting the public. While there is an understandable desire for punishment and justice for victims, focusing purely on punishment does little to reduce future crime. Rehabilitation, through education, therapy and job training, addresses the causes and helps offenders become productive citizens, which benefits everyone. So although punishment has its place, I think a system oriented towards rehabilitation is ultimately more constructive and beneficial for society.

Vocabulary: rehabilitation · constructive · beneficial

Q5Future
Do you think technology will help reduce crime in the future?

I think it will play a significant, double-edged role. On one hand, technologies like surveillance, data analysis and improved forensics can help prevent and solve crimes more effectively. On the other hand, technology also enables new forms of crime, such as cybercrime and fraud, which are growing rapidly. There are also privacy concerns with heavy surveillance. So technology will be a powerful tool against crime, but it will also create new vulnerabilities and dilemmas.

Vocabulary: tool · vulnerabilities · dilemmas

Q6Problem & Solution
How can societies reduce crime rates?

A comprehensive approach works best. Tackling the root causes, by reducing poverty and inequality and improving education and employment, addresses why crime occurs. Effective, fair policing and a justice system that rehabilitates rather than merely punishes are important. Strong communities and support for at-risk young people also help. Essentially, combining prevention, opportunity and rehabilitation, rather than relying solely on punishment, offers the most sustainable way to reduce crime.

Vocabulary: prevention · punishment · sustainable

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of harsher punishments for crime?

Proponents argue harsher punishments deter crime and satisfy the public's sense of justice, while keeping dangerous offenders off the streets. However, the evidence that severe punishment actually deters crime is surprisingly weak; the certainty of being caught matters more than the severity. Harsh sentences can also be costly and hinder rehabilitation. So while there is emotional appeal, I am sceptical that simply being harsher is effective; addressing root causes is more productive.

Vocabulary: harsher · effective · productive

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say poverty is the main cause of crime. Do you agree?

I partly agree, as there is a clear correlation, but it is not the whole story. Poverty and the desperation, lack of opportunity and inequality it brings undoubtedly create fertile ground for crime. However, most poor people are law-abiding, and crime also occurs among the wealthy, such as fraud. So while poverty is a significant contributing factor, crime is a complex phenomenon with multiple causes, and attributing it solely to poverty is an oversimplification of a multifaceted issue.

Vocabulary: contributing · oversimplification · multifaceted

7. Government & Public Policy

The role of government and how societies are governed.

Q1Opinion
What do you think is the most important role of a government?

In my view, the most fundamental role of a government is to protect its citizens and ensure their basic wellbeing. This includes maintaining security and the rule of law, but also providing essential public services like healthcare, education and infrastructure. Beyond that, a good government should create the conditions for people to thrive and prosper fairly. So I see the core role as safeguarding both the security and the welfare of its people.

Vocabulary: security · welfare · people

Q2Comparison
Should governments focus more on the economy or on social welfare?

I believe these two are deeply interconnected rather than opposed. A strong economy generates the wealth needed to fund social welfare, while investing in people's health, education and wellbeing actually strengthens the economy by creating a productive workforce. Neglecting either ultimately harms the other. So rather than choosing one over the other, I think a wise government pursues both in tandem, recognising that economic prosperity and social welfare are mutually reinforcing.

Vocabulary: welfare · mutually · reinforcing

Q3Cause & Effect
What are the effects of good governance on a country?

Good governance has profoundly positive effects. Transparent, accountable and effective government fosters economic growth, attracts investment and reduces corruption. It ensures public services function well and that resources are used fairly, which improves citizens' quality of life. It also builds public trust and social stability. Conversely, poor governance breeds corruption and stagnation. So good governance is genuinely the foundation of a country's prosperity, stability and overall development.

Vocabulary: foundation · prosperity · development

Q4Social Impact
How much should governments intervene in people's lives?

This is a matter of constant debate, and I believe in a balanced approach. Governments should intervene to protect citizens, provide essential services and correct market failures, as some intervention is clearly necessary for a functioning society. However, excessive intervention can stifle freedom, innovation and personal responsibility. So the ideal lies somewhere in between; the government should provide a strong safety net and framework while respecting individual autonomy and avoiding becoming overbearing or paternalistic.

Vocabulary: autonomy · overbearing · paternalistic

Q5Future
How do you think the role of government will change in the future?

I anticipate governments will face new and complex challenges that reshape their role. Issues like climate change, artificial intelligence, and global pandemics require unprecedented coordination and regulation. Technology may enable more direct citizen participation but also greater surveillance. Governments will likely need to become more agile and collaborative internationally. So while the fundamental role of serving citizens remains, the tools and challenges will evolve, demanding greater adaptability from those who govern.

Vocabulary: agile · evolve · adaptability

Q6Problem & Solution
How can governments earn the trust of their citizens?

Trust is earned through transparency, accountability and competence. Governments that are open about their decisions, deliver on their promises, and tackle corruption tend to be trusted. Actually improving people's lives through effective policy is crucial. Listening to citizens and involving them in decisions fosters a sense of ownership. So by being transparent, honest and genuinely responsive to people's needs, governments can rebuild and maintain the public trust that is so vital to good governance.

Vocabulary: transparent · trust · vital

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of a strong central government?

A strong central government can act decisively, implement coherent national policy, and coordinate effectively during crises, which is valuable. It can also ensure consistency and tackle large-scale problems. The disadvantages are that too much centralised power can become authoritarian, ignore local needs, and stifle regional autonomy and diversity. It may also be less responsive. So while strong central authority has clear benefits, it must be balanced with checks and local representation to avoid overreach.

Vocabulary: benefits · representation · overreach

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say governments should provide free healthcare for everyone. Do you agree?

I strongly agree with this principle. I believe access to healthcare is a fundamental human right, not a privilege dependent on wealth. Universal healthcare ensures that no one is denied treatment or bankrupted by illness, which is both humane and promotes a healthier, more productive society. While it is expensive and requires efficient management, the alternative, where people suffer for lack of money, seems deeply unjust. So I consider free, universal healthcare a worthwhile and civilised investment.

Vocabulary: unjust · civilised · investment

8. Ethics & Moral Values

Right and wrong, moral responsibility, and how values are formed.

Q1Opinion
Where do you think people get their moral values from?

I believe our moral values come from a combination of sources. Our upbringing and family are hugely influential, instilling early notions of right and wrong. Culture, religion and society also shape our ethics, as do our own experiences and reflection as we mature. Some argue there is even an innate, universal sense of fairness. So our values are essentially absorbed from those around us and then refined through personal reflection over time.

Vocabulary: absorbed · refined · reflection

Q2Comparison
Are moral values the same across all cultures?

I think there is a fascinating mix of universal and culturally specific values. Certain core principles, like prohibitions against murder or valuing honesty and care for others, appear across virtually all cultures, suggesting a shared moral foundation. However, many values, such as attitudes to family, individualism or specific customs, vary considerably between cultures. So while there are some universal moral constants, ethics are also significantly shaped by cultural context.

Vocabulary: universal · constants · shaped

Q3Cause & Effect
What happens to a society when it loses its moral values?

A society that loses its shared moral compass tends to suffer serious consequences. Trust between people erodes, corruption and dishonesty can flourish, and social cohesion breaks down. Without agreed ethical standards, laws alone struggle to maintain order, and self-interest can dominate at the expense of the common good. So a strong shared morality acts as invisible social glue; its erosion can lead to division, distrust and dysfunction.

Vocabulary: glue · division · distrust

Q4Social Impact
Do you think moral values are declining in modern society?

This is a common concern, but I am somewhat sceptical. Every generation seems to believe that values are declining, yet by many measures, such as the reduction of violence and expansion of rights, society has become more ethical over time. What is really happening, I think, is that values are shifting rather than declining; some traditional values fade while new ones, like tolerance and environmental awareness, emerge. So I would say values are evolving, not simply deteriorating or vanishing.

Vocabulary: evolving · deteriorating · vanishing

Q5Future
How might moral values change in the future?

I expect our moral frameworks to be tested and expanded by new challenges. Advances in technology, such as artificial intelligence and genetic engineering, will raise entirely new ethical dilemmas we have never faced before. Growing awareness may also extend our moral concern further, perhaps towards animals, the environment and future generations. So I anticipate that ethics will continue to evolve, becoming broader in scope as we grapple with unprecedented moral questions.

Vocabulary: evolve · grapple · questions

Q6Problem & Solution
How can strong moral values be encouraged in young people?

The most powerful way is through example, since children learn ethics primarily by observing the adults around them. Parents and teachers who model integrity, kindness and honesty have a profound influence. Open discussion of ethical issues, encouraging empathy, and involving young people in helping others also help. Rather than simply lecturing, allowing them to reason through moral situations builds genuine understanding. So values are best instilled through consistent example and guided reflection.

Vocabulary: instilled · example · guided

Q7Adv & Disadv
Is it always right to follow the rules, or are there times to break them?

While rules are generally important for order and fairness, I do not believe following them is always morally right. History shows that some laws have been deeply unjust, and challenging them, through civil disobedience, has been essential to progress. There are times when a higher moral principle justifies breaking an unjust rule. However, this is a serious matter, not an excuse for convenience. So rules should generally be respected, but blind obedience can sometimes conflict with genuine morality.

Vocabulary: obedience · conflict · morality

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say people are becoming more selfish. Do you agree?

I am not entirely convinced. It is a common perception, and certain trends, like consumerism and individualism, can appear to encourage selfishness. However, I see abundant evidence of generosity, volunteering and collective action, particularly among young people concerned with social and environmental causes. Human nature contains both selfish and altruistic tendencies. So rather than people simply becoming more selfish, I think it is a mischaracterisation; both generosity and self-interest coexist as they always have.

Vocabulary: mischaracterisation · generosity · have

9. Global Challenges

Problems that cross borders and require international cooperation.

Q1Opinion
What do you think is the biggest challenge facing the world today?

While there are many pressing issues, I believe climate change is arguably the most significant, because it threatens the very habitability of our planet and amplifies other problems like poverty and conflict. Its effects are global and potentially irreversible. That said, inequality and the risks of new technology are also profound. So although I would single out climate change as the most urgent and far-reaching, these challenges are deeply interconnected.

Vocabulary: urgent · far-reaching · interconnected

Q2Comparison
Should countries solve their own problems or work together on global issues?

For many modern challenges, international cooperation is not just preferable but essential. Problems like climate change, pandemics and terrorism do not respect borders, so no single country can solve them alone. While nations should certainly address their domestic issues, the great transnational challenges require collective, coordinated action. So I would argue that in an interconnected world, a spirit of cooperation and shared responsibility is indispensable for tackling our common threats.

Vocabulary: transnational · cooperation · responsibility

Q3Cause & Effect
Why is it so difficult to solve global problems?

The difficulty largely stems from competing national interests and a lack of trust. Countries are often reluctant to bear costs or make sacrifices, especially if they fear others will not do the same. Differing priorities between rich and poor nations complicate matters, and there is no single global authority to enforce agreements. Short-term political thinking also gets in the way. So global problems persist because collective action requires overcoming deep-seated self-interest and coordination failures.

Vocabulary: self-interest · coordination · failures

Q4Social Impact
How do global problems affect ordinary people?

Global problems have very real and personal impacts, even when they seem distant. Climate change brings extreme weather that destroys homes and livelihoods. A global pandemic, as we have seen, can upend everyone's daily life and health. Economic crises that originate elsewhere can cause job losses locally. So although these issues are global in scale, their consequences are felt intimately in ordinary people's livelihoods, health and security, which is why they matter to us all.

Vocabulary: livelihoods · health · security

Q5Future
Are you optimistic that the world can solve its biggest problems?

I try to remain cautiously optimistic, despite the daunting challenges. History shows that humanity is remarkably capable of innovation and cooperation when truly pressed, and we have overcome major threats before. Growing global awareness and technological progress offer real hope. However, success is far from guaranteed and will require unprecedented political will. So while I acknowledge the risks are serious, I choose to believe in human ingenuity and our capacity to rise to the occasion.

Vocabulary: ingenuity · rise · occasion

Q6Problem & Solution
How can international cooperation on global issues be improved?

Strengthening international institutions and agreements is essential, giving them more authority to coordinate and, where necessary, enforce collective action. Building trust between nations through dialogue and fair burden-sharing is crucial, ensuring poorer countries are supported. Aligning short-term incentives with long-term goals would help too. Ultimately, fostering a genuine sense of shared humanity and common purpose, over narrow nationalism, is the foundation of effective cooperation.

Vocabulary: humanity · purpose · cooperation

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of an interconnected world?

The advantages of our interconnected world are immense: the free flow of ideas, trade and technology, cultural exchange, and the ability to cooperate on shared problems. It has driven progress and lifted many from poverty. The disadvantages are that problems, whether financial crises, pandemics or misinformation, also spread rapidly across borders, making us more vulnerable. So interconnection brings both tremendous opportunity and shared vulnerability, which we must learn to manage wisely.

Vocabulary: vulnerable · opportunity · vulnerability

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say ordinary individuals cannot influence global issues. Do you agree?

I respectfully disagree, as I find this view unduly disempowering. While it is true that individuals cannot single-handedly solve global problems, collective individual action is precisely what drives change. Our consumer choices, our activism, and above all our votes shape the policies of governments and the behaviour of corporations. Great social movements have always begun with ordinary people. So while one person's impact is small, collectively individuals wield genuine influence and should not underestimate it.

Vocabulary: collectively · influence · underestimate
