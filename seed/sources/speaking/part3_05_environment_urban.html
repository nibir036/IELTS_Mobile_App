1. Environmental Protection

Individual and collective responsibility for protecting the environment.

Q1Opinion
Whose responsibility is it to protect the environment?

I believe it is a shared responsibility, though the burden falls unevenly. Individuals certainly have a role through their daily choices, but honestly, their impact is limited compared to that of governments and large corporations. Governments must implement effective policy and regulation, while industries must change their practices. So while everyone should do their part, I think the greatest responsibility lies with those who hold the power to enact systemic change.

Vocabulary: power · systemic · change

Q2Comparison
Are individuals or governments more effective at protecting the environment?

While individual actions are valuable and add up, I would argue governments are far more effective due to their scale of influence. A single person recycling makes a small difference, but a government can regulate entire industries, invest in renewable energy and shape behaviour through law. That said, individual pressure and voting ultimately influence what governments do. So the two are interconnected, but the leverage of government is undeniably greater.

Vocabulary: ultimately · interconnected · leverage

Q3Cause & Effect
Why do you think some people do not care about the environment?

There are several reasons. For some, environmental problems feel distant and abstract, so they do not feel a sense of urgency. Others prioritise immediate economic concerns, like putting food on the table, over long-term issues. There is also a feeling of helplessness, that individual action is futile. Additionally, some are simply unaware or misinformed. So it is usually a mix of apathy, immediate priorities and a sense of powerlessness.

Vocabulary: helplessness · futile · apathy

Q4Social Impact
How does environmental damage affect people's lives?

The effects are increasingly tangible and severe. Pollution directly harms public health, causing respiratory and other illnesses. Climate change brings extreme weather, threatening homes, food security and livelihoods, especially for the poor. The degradation of natural resources also affects economies and can even fuel conflict over scarce resources. So environmental damage is not an abstract issue; it has profound and growing repercussions for human health, prosperity and stability.

Vocabulary: repercussions · health · prosperity

Q5Future
Are you optimistic about the future of the environment?

I am cautiously optimistic, though the challenges are daunting. On one hand, the scale of problems like climate change is alarming, and progress often feels too slow. On the other hand, awareness has never been higher, technology in renewable energy is advancing rapidly, and younger generations are deeply committed to the cause. So while I have real concerns, I believe human ingenuity and growing collective will give us genuine grounds for hope.

Vocabulary: committed · ingenuity · hope

Q6Problem & Solution
What can ordinary people do to help the environment?

There is a surprising amount individuals can do. Reducing consumption and waste, recycling, and being mindful of energy and water use all help. Choosing sustainable transport, like walking or public transit, and eating less meat can significantly cut one's carbon footprint. Beyond personal habits, supporting green businesses and, crucially, voting for environmentally responsible policies amplifies our impact. Collectively, these individual choices can drive genuine change.

Vocabulary: amplifies · choices · change

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of strict environmental laws?

The advantages are clear: strict laws can effectively curb pollution, protect natural resources and drive innovation in clean technology, safeguarding public health and the planet. The potential disadvantages are economic; such regulations can increase costs for businesses and consumers and may, in the short term, affect certain industries and jobs. However, I believe the long-term benefits far outweigh these costs, though a balanced, well-planned transition is essential.

Vocabulary: balanced · transition · essential

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say economic growth is more important than protecting the environment. Do you agree?

I strongly disagree with framing them as opposites. This is a short-sighted view, because a healthy environment is actually the foundation of long-term economic prosperity; you cannot have a thriving economy on a ruined planet. Furthermore, the green economy itself is a huge source of growth and jobs. So rather than a trade-off, I believe sustainable development, where growth and protection go hand in hand, is not only possible but imperative and mutually reinforcing.

Vocabulary: imperative · mutually · reinforcing

2. Climate Change

Global warming, its causes, and the response to it.

Q1Opinion
How serious a problem do you think climate change is?

I consider it one of the most serious challenges facing humanity, if not the most serious. The scientific consensus is overwhelming, and we are already witnessing its effects through more frequent extreme weather, rising sea levels and disrupted ecosystems. Its potential to cause widespread displacement, food insecurity and conflict is genuinely alarming. So while some still downplay it, I believe it is an existential threat that demands urgent, decisive action.

Vocabulary: existential · urgent · decisive

Q2Comparison
Do you think richer or poorer countries should do more to fight climate change?

This is a matter of both responsibility and capability. I believe richer, industrialised nations bear a greater responsibility, as they have contributed the most historical emissions and have the financial resources to lead. It seems only fair for them to do more, including helping poorer nations. However, developing countries, some of which are now major emitters, must also participate. So the burden should be shared equitably, with the wealthy shouldering a proportionate load.

Vocabulary: equitably · wealthy · proportionate

Q3Cause & Effect
What are the main effects of climate change on people?

The effects on people are wide-ranging and increasingly severe. More intense heatwaves, floods and storms directly threaten lives and homes. Changing weather patterns disrupt agriculture, jeopardising food security and livelihoods. Rising seas endanger coastal communities, potentially creating millions of climate refugees. There are also serious health impacts. So climate change is not merely an environmental issue; it is a profound humanitarian one that threatens the stability of entire societies.

Vocabulary: threatens · stability · societies

Q4Social Impact
Why is it difficult for countries to agree on climate action?

The difficulty lies in competing national interests. Countries worry that ambitious climate action will harm their own economies and competitiveness, so many are reluctant to act unless others do too. There are also disputes over who is most responsible and who should bear the costs. Powerful industries lobby against change as well. So despite the shared threat, short-term self-interest and a lack of trust often hinder the global cooperation required.

Vocabulary: self-interest · trust · hinder

Q5Future
Do you think we can still prevent the worst effects of climate change?

I believe we still can, but the window is closing rapidly and it will require unprecedented effort. The technology and knowledge to transition to a clean economy largely exist; what is often lacking is the political will and global cooperation. If we act decisively now, we can still limit the damage, though some impacts are already inevitable. So I remain hopeful, but only if we treat it with the urgency it truly warrants.

Vocabulary: inevitable · urgency · warrants

Q6Problem & Solution
What are the most effective ways to tackle climate change?

The most effective solutions involve rapidly transitioning away from fossil fuels towards renewable energy like solar and wind. Improving energy efficiency and protecting and restoring forests, which absorb carbon, are also crucial. Government policy is key, from carbon pricing to investment in green technology. Individual lifestyle changes help too. Essentially, it requires a coordinated overhaul of our energy, transport and consumption systems on a global scale.

Vocabulary: overhaul · global · scale

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of switching to renewable energy?

The advantages are compelling: renewables are clean, produce no carbon emissions, and are increasingly cheap and inexhaustible, enhancing energy security. They also create new jobs. The disadvantages include the high initial cost of infrastructure, and the fact that sources like solar and wind are intermittent, requiring storage solutions. Transitioning also affects existing fossil fuel industries. So while there are real hurdles, the long-term benefits make it a clearly worthwhile shift.

Vocabulary: intermittent · hurdles · worthwhile

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say individuals can do little about climate change. Do you agree?

I partly agree but find the statement disempowering. It is true that systemic change by governments and industry is far more impactful than individual action alone, so no one should feel the entire burden rests on them. However, I disagree that individuals are powerless. Collectively, our consumer choices, our voices and, above all, our votes exert real pressure that drives the bigger changes. So individual action is not futile; it is part of a larger momentum.

Vocabulary: drives · futile · momentum

3. Urban Development

The growth of cities and the challenges of urban living.

Q1Opinion
Do you think living in a city is better than living in the countryside?

It really depends on one's priorities and stage of life. Cities offer better job opportunities, amenities, healthcare and a vibrant social life, which many find appealing. The countryside, by contrast, offers peace, clean air, space and a stronger sense of community. Personally, I appreciate the convenience of the city for now, but I can see the profound appeal of a quieter rural life later. So neither is objectively better; each suits different needs and temperaments.

Vocabulary: convenience · needs · temperaments

Q2Comparison
How have cities changed over the past few decades?

Cities have transformed dramatically. They have grown enormously in size and population due to rapid urbanisation, becoming denser and often more congested. Skylines have risen with skyscrapers, and technology has made them smarter. However, this growth has often outpaced infrastructure, worsening problems like traffic, pollution and housing shortages. So while cities have become more developed and connected, this expansion has brought significant growing pains.

Vocabulary: developed · connected · pains

Q3Cause & Effect
Why do so many people move to cities?

The primary driver is economic opportunity. Cities are hubs of employment, offering far more and better-paid jobs than rural areas, which is the biggest pull factor. They also provide superior access to education, healthcare and services. For many, the appeal of a more exciting, diverse lifestyle is also a draw. So this migration is largely driven by the search for a better standard of living, though it can place enormous strain on urban infrastructure and resources.

Vocabulary: strain · infrastructure · resources

Q4Social Impact
What are the main problems facing large cities today?

Large cities grapple with numerous interconnected challenges. Traffic congestion and air pollution are among the most visible, harming both quality of life and health. A shortage of affordable housing is another major issue, pushing up costs and inequality. Overcrowded public services, waste management and, in some cases, rising crime add to the burden. So while cities are engines of prosperity, managing this rapid growth sustainably is a formidable challenge.

Vocabulary: rapid · sustainably · challenge

Q5Future
What do you think cities will be like in the future?

I envision cities becoming smarter and, hopefully, greener. Technology will likely manage traffic, energy and services more efficiently through data. There is a strong push towards more sustainable, walkable cities with more green spaces and cleaner transport. However, they may also become even more crowded. So I am hopeful the cities of the future will be more liveable and sustainable, provided planning prioritises people and the environment over pure expansion.

Vocabulary: liveable · sustainable · prioritises

Q6Problem & Solution
How can cities be made better places to live?

Thoughtful urban planning is the key. Investing in efficient, affordable public transport reduces congestion and pollution. Creating more green spaces and pedestrian areas improves wellbeing. Ensuring an adequate supply of affordable housing tackles inequality. Designing mixed-use, walkable neighbourhoods fosters community. Essentially, cities become better when they are planned around the needs of people and the environment, rather than simply cars and commerce.

Vocabulary: people · environment · cars

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of urbanisation?

Urbanisation drives economic growth and innovation, and provides better access to jobs, services and opportunities, lifting many out of poverty. Cities are also cultural and intellectual hubs. However, the disadvantages include overcrowding, pollution, pressure on housing and infrastructure, and the loss of green space and community ties. It can also deepen inequality. So urbanisation is a powerful engine of progress, but one that must be managed to avoid serious social and environmental costs.

Vocabulary: deepen · progress · costs

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say governments should stop cities from growing too big. Do you agree?

I do not think halting growth is realistic or desirable, but managing it is essential. Trying to stop urban growth outright would be difficult and could stifle economic opportunity. However, I strongly agree that governments should guide this growth intelligently, perhaps by developing smaller satellite cities and improving rural areas to ease the pressure. So rather than restricting cities, the focus should be on making growth sustainable and balanced.

Vocabulary: pressure · restricting · sustainable

4. Transportation

How people get around and the future of mobility.

Q1Opinion
Do you think public transport should be free?

I find the idea appealing, though it is complex. Making public transport free could reduce traffic, cut pollution and help lower-income people enormously, which are compelling social benefits. However, it would be extremely expensive to fund and might lead to overcrowding or reduced service quality without careful management. So while I support heavily subsidising it to keep it affordable, whether it should be entirely free depends on a country's priorities and budget.

Vocabulary: subsidising · priorities · budget

Q2Comparison
Is it better to travel by public transport or private car?

Each has its merits, but from a societal viewpoint, public transport is clearly preferable. It is far more environmentally friendly, reduces congestion, and is cheaper for the user. Private cars, however, offer unmatched convenience, comfort and flexibility, especially where public transport is poor. So while I personally value the freedom of a car, I believe well-developed public transport is the more sustainable and equitable option for society as a whole.

Vocabulary: sustainable · equitable · whole

Q3Cause & Effect
What are the effects of traffic congestion on cities?

Traffic congestion has serious and costly effects. It wastes an enormous amount of people's time, reducing productivity and quality of life. It significantly worsens air pollution, harming public health and the environment. It also causes frustration and stress for commuters and can hamper emergency services. Economically, the lost hours and fuel represent a huge drain. So congestion is far more than an inconvenience; it undermines a city's efficiency and wellbeing.

Vocabulary: drain · undermines · efficiency

Q4Social Impact
How does good transport benefit a society?

An efficient transport system is the backbone of a thriving society. It connects people to jobs, education and services, enhancing opportunity and social mobility. It enables the smooth flow of goods, boosting the economy. Good public transport, in particular, reduces pollution and congestion while providing affordable mobility for all, including those without cars. So investing in transport is not just about convenience; it is fundamental to economic prosperity and social inclusion.

Vocabulary: fundamental · prosperity · inclusion

Q5Future
How do you think transport will change in the future?

I anticipate significant transformation. Electric vehicles will likely become the norm, drastically cutting emissions, and autonomous, self-driving cars may reshape how we travel. Cities will probably invest more in cycling, walking and clean public transport. Concepts like shared mobility could reduce car ownership. So the future of transport is likely to be cleaner, smarter and more integrated, hopefully making our cities less polluted and more liveable.

Vocabulary: integrated · polluted · liveable

Q6Problem & Solution
How can traffic problems in cities be solved?

A combination of measures is most effective. Investing heavily in reliable, affordable public transport gives people a genuine alternative to driving. Encouraging cycling and walking through better infrastructure helps too. Some cities use congestion charging to discourage car use in busy areas. Better urban planning that reduces the need to travel far is also valuable. Essentially, the solution lies in making sustainable options more attractive than driving, rather than simply widening roads.

Vocabulary: attractive · widening · roads

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of owning a car?

Owning a car offers tremendous freedom, convenience and comfort, allowing you to travel on your own schedule, which is especially valuable where public transport is limited. The disadvantages, however, are significant: the high costs of purchase, fuel, insurance and maintenance, plus the stress of traffic and parking. There is also the considerable environmental impact. So a car provides genuine autonomy, but at a substantial financial and ecological cost.

Vocabulary: impact · autonomy · ecological

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say cars should be banned from city centres. Do you agree?

I broadly agree with this, at least for private cars in the busiest central areas. Many cities that have pedestrianised their centres have seen reduced pollution, safer streets and more vibrant public life, which is very appealing. However, it must be done carefully, ensuring excellent public transport alternatives and access for deliveries and those with disabilities. So rather than a blanket ban, I support significantly restricting cars to create more liveable, people-friendly centres.

Vocabulary: restricting · liveable · people-friendly

5. Pollution

Air, water, and other pollution and its impact on health and nature.

Q1Opinion
How serious is the problem of pollution in your country?

Unfortunately, pollution is a very serious problem in my country, particularly in the major cities. Air quality often reaches hazardous levels due to traffic, industry and construction, which poses a real threat to public health. Water pollution from industrial and domestic waste is also a significant concern. So while there is growing awareness, I would say pollution remains one of our most pressing environmental and public-health challenges.

Vocabulary: environmental · public-health · challenges

Q2Comparison
Which do you think is worse, air pollution or water pollution?

Both are extremely damaging, and it is hard to rank them. Air pollution is perhaps more immediate and inescapable, as we breathe constantly, and it causes widespread respiratory illness. Water pollution, however, is arguably more insidious, contaminating our drinking water and food chain and devastating aquatic ecosystems. So rather than one being definitively worse, I would say they are both severe problems with different but equally devastating consequences.

Vocabulary: severe · devastating · consequences

Q3Cause & Effect
What are the main causes of pollution in cities?

Urban pollution stems from several sources. Vehicle emissions are a primary culprit, given the sheer density of traffic. Industrial activity and factories release pollutants into the air and water. Construction generates dust, and the burning of fossil fuels for energy contributes heavily. Poor waste management adds to the problem. So it is largely a by-product of dense population, industry and reliance on fossil fuels concentrated in a small area.

Vocabulary: reliance · fossil · fuels

Q4Social Impact
How does pollution affect people's health?

The health impacts of pollution are profound and well-documented. Air pollution causes and worsens respiratory conditions like asthma, as well as heart disease and even cancer, contributing to millions of premature deaths globally. Water pollution can cause serious diseases through contaminated drinking water. Vulnerable groups, like children and the elderly, are especially at risk. So pollution is a genuine silent killer, imposing an enormous burden on public health.

Vocabulary: silent · burden · health

Q5Future
Do you think pollution will get better or worse in the future?

I am cautiously hopeful it will improve, though it depends on our actions. On one hand, rapid industrialisation in developing nations could worsen it in the short term. On the other, growing awareness, stricter regulations, and cleaner technologies like electric vehicles and renewable energy offer real solutions. Many developed cities have already reduced pollution significantly. So with sufficient political will and innovation, I believe we can reverse the trend, but it is not guaranteed.

Vocabulary: innovation · reverse · guaranteed

Q6Problem & Solution
What can be done to reduce pollution?

Tackling pollution requires action on multiple fronts. Transitioning to renewable energy and electric transport would drastically cut emissions. Enforcing strict regulations on industrial waste and improving public transport are vital. Better waste management and recycling reduce landfill and litter. On an individual level, reducing consumption and waste helps. Ultimately, a combination of regulation, clean technology and changed behaviour offers the most effective path forward.

Vocabulary: regulation · technology · behaviour

Q7Adv & Disadv
Some solutions to pollution are expensive. Are they worth the cost?

I firmly believe they are worth it, viewed correctly. While measures like transitioning to clean energy carry a high upfront cost, this must be weighed against the enormous, often hidden, costs of pollution itself, from healthcare bills to environmental damage and lost productivity. In truth, the cost of inaction is far greater. So although the investment is substantial, it is far more prudent than allowing pollution to escalate unchecked.

Vocabulary: inaction · prudent · escalate

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say factories should be moved away from residential areas. Do you agree?

I largely agree with this as a sensible measure. Locating polluting factories away from where people live can significantly reduce residents' direct exposure to harmful air and water pollution, protecting public health. However, it is not a complete solution, as pollution still spreads, and it may simply shift the burden to another community. So while separating industry from homes is beneficial, it must be combined with actually reducing the pollution at its source.

Vocabulary: beneficial · reducing · source

6. Sustainability

Living within environmental limits and sustainable development.

Q1Opinion
What does it mean to live sustainably?

To me, living sustainably means meeting our own needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. In practice, it involves being mindful of our consumption, reducing waste, and using resources efficiently and responsibly. It is about living within the planet's ecological limits rather than depleting them. Essentially, it is a shift from a throwaway, exploitative mindset to one of stewardship and long-term responsibility.

Vocabulary: throwaway · stewardship · responsibility

Q2Comparison
Are people more environmentally conscious now than in the past?

Yes, considerably so. Environmental awareness has grown enormously over recent decades, driven by scientific evidence, media coverage and visible impacts like extreme weather. Concepts like recycling, carbon footprints and sustainability are now part of everyday conversation, which was not the case a generation ago. However, I would add that awareness does not always translate into action. So people are certainly more conscious, even if their behaviour has not fully caught up.

Vocabulary: translate · conscious · behaviour

Q3Cause & Effect
Why is it hard for people to change to a more sustainable lifestyle?

Several barriers make it difficult. Sustainable options are sometimes more expensive or less convenient than the alternatives, so cost and habit get in the way. Many people also feel their individual actions are insignificant against such a vast problem. Additionally, our entire economic system is built around consumption, making unsustainable choices the default. So it is a combination of practical obstacles, ingrained habits and a sense of powerlessness.

Vocabulary: insignificant · practical · powerlessness

Q4Social Impact
How can businesses become more sustainable?

Businesses have a huge role to play. They can reduce their carbon footprint by using renewable energy and improving efficiency. Adopting circular economy principles, where products are designed to be reused and recycled, cuts waste dramatically. Sourcing materials ethically and being transparent about their impact matters too. Crucially, they should see sustainability not as a cost but as an investment that can drive innovation and win loyalty from increasingly conscious consumers.

Vocabulary: investment · innovation · loyalty

Q5Future
Do you think future generations will live more sustainably?

I am hopeful they will, partly out of necessity. As the impacts of unsustainable living become more severe, future generations will likely be forced to adapt. Encouragingly, young people today already show strong environmental values and are pushing for change. Advances in green technology should also make sustainable living easier and cheaper. So I believe sustainability will increasingly become the norm rather than the exception, driven by both necessity and shifting values.

Vocabulary: norm · necessity · values

Q6Problem & Solution
How can governments encourage sustainable behaviour?

Governments have powerful tools at their disposal. They can use financial incentives, like subsidies for renewable energy or electric cars, and taxes on pollution and waste to steer behaviour. Investing in sustainable infrastructure, such as public transport and recycling, makes green choices easier. Regulation and clear information, like carbon labelling, also help. Essentially, by making the sustainable choice the easy, affordable and default option, governments can drive widespread change.

Vocabulary: easy · affordable · change

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of recycling?

Recycling has clear advantages: it conserves natural resources, reduces landfill waste, saves energy compared to making new products, and cuts pollution. It also raises environmental awareness. The disadvantages are that the process itself consumes energy and water, some materials are difficult or costly to recycle, and contamination can render efforts ineffective. So while recycling is genuinely beneficial, it is not a complete solution and works best alongside reducing and reusing.

Vocabulary: ineffective · beneficial · reusing

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say sustainable products are too expensive for ordinary people. Do you agree?

There is some truth to this currently, though it is changing. It is true that some sustainable products, like organic food or electric cars, carry a higher price tag, which can put them out of reach for those on tight budgets. This is a real barrier to widespread adoption. However, as demand grows and technology improves, prices are steadily falling. So while cost is a genuine obstacle today, I believe sustainability is becoming increasingly affordable and accessible.

Vocabulary: obstacle · affordable · accessible

7. Housing

Affordable housing, urban living space, and the property market.

Q1Opinion
Do you think everyone has the right to affordable housing?

I strongly believe that access to decent, affordable housing is a fundamental human right, not a privilege. Shelter is a basic need, and stable housing is the foundation upon which people build healthy, productive lives. When housing becomes unaffordable, it drives inequality, homelessness and hardship. So I think governments have a clear responsibility to ensure that housing is available and affordable to all, treating it as an essential rather than a mere commodity.

Vocabulary: affordable · essential · commodity

Q2Comparison
Is it better to rent or to own a home?

Both have their advantages, depending on circumstances. Owning a home offers stability, security and the chance to build wealth over time, which is why many aspire to it. Renting, however, offers greater flexibility and freedom from the burdens of maintenance and large mortgages, which suits those who value mobility. So there is no universal answer; it depends on one's financial situation, life stage and how much stability versus flexibility one prioritises.

Vocabulary: stability · flexibility · prioritises

Q3Cause & Effect
Why is housing so expensive in many cities?

The high cost is driven by a fundamental imbalance between supply and demand. As people flock to cities for work, demand for housing soars, while the supply of new homes often fails to keep pace due to limited land and slow construction. Property speculation and foreign investment can also inflate prices. So it is largely a matter of high demand meeting constrained supply, made worse by speculation, pushing prices beyond ordinary people's reach.

Vocabulary: constrained · speculation · reach

Q4Social Impact
How does a housing shortage affect society?

A housing shortage has far-reaching social consequences. It drives up rents and prices, forcing people to spend a huge portion of their income on housing, which deepens financial stress and inequality. It can lead to overcrowding, longer commutes, and in severe cases, homelessness. It may also prevent young people from becoming independent. So a lack of affordable housing genuinely undermines social mobility and the overall wellbeing of a community.

Vocabulary: undermines · mobility · wellbeing

Q5Future
How do you think housing will change in the future?

I anticipate several developments. To address shortages and sustainability, we may see more compact, high-density and eco-friendly housing, along with innovative solutions like modular or prefabricated homes. Smart-home technology will likely become standard. There may also be a shift towards co-living arrangements, especially in expensive cities. So future housing may become smaller and denser, but hopefully smarter, greener and more affordable, adapting to modern needs.

Vocabulary: affordable · adapting · needs

Q6Problem & Solution
What can be done to make housing more affordable?

Increasing the supply of housing is fundamental, particularly affordable and social housing built by governments. Reforming planning regulations to allow more construction can help. Measures to curb speculation, such as taxes on empty properties, may also cool prices. Rent controls and support for first-time buyers are other options. Essentially, treating housing as a basic need rather than a pure investment vehicle is key to keeping it accessible and fair.

Vocabulary: investment · accessible · fair

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of living in an apartment versus a house?

Apartments are generally more affordable, low-maintenance, and often centrally located with good security, which is convenient. The disadvantages are less space and privacy, no garden, and potential noise from neighbours. Houses, by contrast, offer more space, privacy and outdoor areas, ideal for families, but they are more expensive and require more upkeep. So it is really a trade-off between convenience and affordability on one hand, and space and privacy on the other.

Vocabulary: convenience · space · privacy

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say owning a home is no longer a realistic goal for young people. Do you agree?

I partly agree, especially in expensive cities, where it has become genuinely difficult. Soaring property prices relative to wages, combined with the challenge of saving for a large deposit, have put homeownership out of reach for many young people, which is a real and worrying shift. However, I would not say it is impossible everywhere. So while it has certainly become far more challenging and requires greater sacrifice, I would not call it entirely unattainable.

Vocabulary: challenging · sacrifice · unattainable

8. Public Spaces

Parks, community areas, and shared spaces in cities.

Q1Opinion
How important are public spaces like parks in a city?

I consider them absolutely essential to a healthy city. Public spaces like parks provide vital areas for recreation, exercise and relaxation, which is crucial for physical and mental wellbeing amid the stress of urban life. They also act as the lungs of a city, improving air quality, and serve as places where the community comes together. So far from being a luxury, well-designed public spaces are fundamental to a liveable and cohesive city.

Vocabulary: fundamental · liveable · cohesive

Q2Comparison
Do you think there are enough public spaces in modern cities?

In many rapidly growing cities, unfortunately not. As urban land becomes increasingly valuable, green and public spaces are often sacrificed for commercial or residential development, which I find short-sighted. Older, well-planned cities tend to have generous parks, whereas newer, denser developments frequently lack them. So while the importance of public space is increasingly recognised, in practice many cities are falling short, prioritising profit over liveability.

Vocabulary: short · profit · liveability

Q3Cause & Effect
What effect do green spaces have on people living in cities?

The effects are overwhelmingly positive and well-researched. Access to green spaces reduces stress, improves mood and encourages physical activity, benefiting both mental and physical health. They provide a peaceful escape from the noise and concrete of the city. Socially, they foster community interaction. Environmentally, they cool cities and clean the air. So green spaces have a genuinely restorative and beneficial effect on urban wellbeing.

Vocabulary: restorative · beneficial · wellbeing

Q4Social Impact
How do public spaces bring communities together?

Public spaces act as vital social hubs. Parks, squares and community centres provide neutral, accessible places where people from all walks of life can meet, interact and mingle, whether at a market, a festival or simply while relaxing. This casual contact builds a sense of community and belonging, and helps break down social barriers. In an increasingly isolated world, these shared spaces are crucial for fostering social cohesion and mutual understanding among diverse people.

Vocabulary: cohesion · understanding · diverse

Q5Future
Do you think cities will have more or fewer public spaces in the future?

I am hopeful there will be more, though it faces real pressure. On one hand, intense competition for urban land threatens existing spaces. On the other, there is a growing recognition of their importance for wellbeing and sustainability, and many forward-thinking cities are now actively creating parks, green roofs and pedestrian zones. So the outcome depends on planning priorities, but I am optimistic that the value of public space will increasingly be recognised and prioritised.

Vocabulary: recognised · prioritised · pressure

Q6Problem & Solution
How can cities create more public spaces despite limited land?

Creativity is key when land is scarce. Cities can repurpose disused areas, such as old railway lines turned into elevated parks, or convert rooftops into green spaces and gardens. Pedestrianising streets reclaims space from cars for people. Even small pocket parks on vacant plots make a difference. So even in dense cities, with imaginative planning, it is possible to integrate valuable public and green space into the existing urban fabric.

Vocabulary: planning · integrate · fabric

Q7Adv & Disadv
Are there any drawbacks to having large public spaces in cities?

While the benefits are enormous, there are a few considerations. Large public spaces are expensive to create and maintain, requiring ongoing public funds. If poorly designed or managed, they can sometimes attract antisocial behaviour or feel unsafe. They also take up valuable land that could be used for housing. However, in my view, these drawbacks are minor and manageable compared to the immense social, health and environmental value such spaces provide.

Vocabulary: manageable · value · provide

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say building more housing is more important than preserving parks. Do you agree?

I understand the argument given the housing crisis, but I largely disagree with sacrificing parks. While housing is undeniably crucial, green spaces are equally vital for residents' health and wellbeing, and destroying them creates a poorer quality of life. It should not be an either-or choice. With clever planning, cities can build housing on brownfield sites and increase density elsewhere while preserving parks. So both are essential, and pitting them against each other is a false dilemma.

Vocabulary: preserving · false · dilemma

9. Wildlife & Conservation

Protecting nature, endangered species, and biodiversity.

Q1Opinion
Why is it important to protect wildlife?

Protecting wildlife is important for several profound reasons. Ecologically, every species plays a role in maintaining the delicate balance of ecosystems that humans ultimately depend on for clean air, water and food. There is also a strong ethical argument that we have a duty to protect other living creatures. Furthermore, biodiversity has immense scientific and economic value. So conservation is not merely sentimental; it is essential for ecological stability and our own survival.

Vocabulary: essential · stability · survival

Q2Comparison
Should we prioritise protecting animals or supporting human development?

I reject the idea that these are truly in opposition, though tensions certainly arise. In the short term, development sometimes conflicts with conservation, and people's immediate needs are understandably a priority. However, in the long run, human development depends entirely on a healthy natural world. Destroying nature for short-term gain is ultimately self-defeating. So rather than choosing one over the other, I believe we must pursue sustainable development that reconciles both imperatives.

Vocabulary: sustainable · reconciles · imperatives

Q3Cause & Effect
What are the main reasons some animal species are becoming extinct?

The primary driver is human activity. Habitat destruction, as forests and wild areas are cleared for agriculture and development, is the biggest threat, leaving animals nowhere to live. Poaching and overfishing directly deplete populations, while pollution and, increasingly, climate change disrupt ecosystems. So extinction is largely a tragic consequence of human encroachment, exploitation and environmental degradation, rather than natural causes.

Vocabulary: encroachment · exploitation · degradation

Q4Social Impact
How does the loss of biodiversity affect humans?

The loss of biodiversity has serious, often underestimated, consequences for humanity. Healthy ecosystems provide us with essential services: pollination for crops, clean water, fertile soil and disease regulation. As species disappear, these systems can collapse, threatening food security and health. We also lose potential sources of medicine and scientific knowledge. So biodiversity loss is not just an environmental tragedy; it directly threatens human livelihoods and resilience.

Vocabulary: threatens · livelihoods · resilience

Q5Future
Are you hopeful about the future of wildlife conservation?

I hold a cautious hope. The threats are severe, and we are losing species at an alarming rate, which is deeply concerning. However, there are genuine success stories where dedicated conservation efforts have brought species back from the brink. Growing public awareness, international cooperation and new technologies for monitoring wildlife are encouraging. So while the challenge is immense, I believe that with sufficient commitment, we can still preserve much of our natural heritage.

Vocabulary: commitment · preserve · heritage

Q6Problem & Solution
What can be done to protect endangered species?

A range of measures is required. Establishing and properly funding protected areas and national parks safeguards vital habitats. Enforcing strict anti-poaching laws and cracking down on illegal wildlife trade is crucial. International cooperation and treaties help protect migratory species. Supporting local communities so they benefit from conservation rather than exploitation is also key. Ultimately, addressing the root causes, like habitat loss and climate change, is essential for lasting protection and recovery.

Vocabulary: protection · recovery · habitats

Q7Adv & Disadv
What are the advantages and disadvantages of zoos?

Zoos have genuine advantages: they play a role in conservation through breeding programmes for endangered species, they educate the public and foster appreciation for wildlife, and they support scientific research. However, the disadvantages are significant ethical concerns about keeping animals in captivity, which can cause them distress and deny them natural behaviour. So while modern, well-run zoos can serve valuable conservation and educational purposes, the welfare of the animals remains a legitimate concern.

Vocabulary: conservation · welfare · concern

Q8Agree / Disagree
Some say humans have no right to interfere with nature. Do you agree?

This is a nuanced issue. I agree we should minimise harmful interference, such as destroying habitats or exploiting species, as we have caused enormous damage. However, I would argue we now have a responsibility to intervene positively, through conservation, to repair the damage we have already done. Given our impact, complete non-interference is neither possible nor helpful. So rather than not interfering at all, we should act as responsible stewards, protecting rather than plundering.

Vocabulary: intervene · stewards · plundering
