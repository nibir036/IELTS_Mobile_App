[0:00] Lecturer: Good afternoon. Today's lecture compares five renewable energy storage technologies.

[0:04] Lecturer: Pumped hydro, lithium-ion batteries, compressed air, flywheels, and hydrogen storage. For each one, I'll explain how it stores energy, its main limitation, a project associated with it, and when the key developments happened. So listen carefully as I go through them in turn.

[0:17] Lecturer: Let's begin with how each technology actually stores energy.

[0:21] Lecturer: Pumped hydro stores energy as water held at high height.

[0:24] Lecturer: When demand is low, water is pumped uphill into a reservoir, and it's released back down through turbines when it's needed.

[0:31] Lecturer: Flywheels store energy as a spinning mass, and because that mass can release its energy almost instantly, flywheels are the technology that responds fastest to sudden demand.

[0:38] Lecturer: Lithium-ion batteries, you already know. They're the technology most common in electric vehicles, as well as in phones and laptops.

[0:44] Lecturer: Compressed air storage works by pumping air into an underground cavern, often an old salt mine, and then releasing it through a turbine when electricity is needed.

[0:52] Lecturer: And hydrogen storage stands out because it can be used for long-distance transport of energy. Hydrogen can be shipped in tankers or sent through pipelines to places other technologies can't reach.

[1:00] Lecturer: Now, each technology also has a significant limitation.

[1:04] Lecturer: Pumped hydro is limited by geography. You need hills or mountains and plenty of water, so it simply can't be built just anywhere.

[1:09] Lecturer: People sometimes raise safety concerns about lithium-ion batteries, but in my view, the real limitation is material scarcity. Lithium and cobalt are in limited supply.

[1:17] Lecturer: Flywheels suffer from short storage duration. They're excellent for smoothing out a brief surge.

[1:20] Lecturer: But they can only hold energy for minutes.

[1:24] Lecturer: Hydrogen's main problem is low efficiency. So much energy is lost converting electricity into hydrogen and back again.

[1:29] Lecturer: And compressed air, suitable caverns exist in more places than you'd think, so the main problem is high cost. These plants are extremely expensive to build.
