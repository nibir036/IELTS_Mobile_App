A Short History of Timekeeping

[0:00] Lecturer: Good afternoon, everyone. Today's lecture traces the history of timekeeping, from the earliest sundials right up to the atomic clocks that keep our modern world running on time.

[0:06] Lecturer: Let's begin with sundials, which were developed by the ancient Egyptians. Their limitation is obvious. They couldn't be used at night or in cloudy weather.

[0:15] Lecturer: One early solution was the water clock, which measured time as water dripped out of a container at a steady rate. Unfortunately, their accuracy was affected by temperature. In colder

[0:23] Lecturer: weather, the water flowed more slowly, and it could even freeze. Hourglasses filled with sand didn't have that problem, and they were especially useful on ships where sailors used them to help calculate their speed.

[0:30] Lecturer: The first mechanical clocks appeared in Europe in the late 13th century.

[0:33] Lecturer: These early clocks were large and expensive, so they were often placed in church towers where the whole community could see and hear them, and they were powered by falling weights. If you'd looked up at one, you might have been surprised by how little it told you.

[0:42] Lecturer: Early clocks had only an hour hand, and they weren't very accurate. They could gain or lose up to 15 minutes a day.

[0:48] Lecturer: A truly transformative moment came when the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock in 1656, making clocks accurate to about a minute a day. However, the motion of ships made pendulum clocks unreliable at sea because the rolling of the ship threw off the regular swing.

[0:58] Lecturer: In the 18th century, John Harrison solved this problem. His marine clock kept accurate time at sea, which allowed sailors to calculate their longitude. On land, meanwhile, towns originally set their clocks to noon, the moment the sun was highest in their own sky, so neighboring towns kept slightly different times.

[1:45] Lecturer: The arrival of the railways made timetables very confusing because every station was working to its own local time.

[1:52] Lecturer: To solve this, in 1884, Greenwich in London was chosen as the prime meridian, leading to a system of 24 time zones, which we still use today.

[2:00] Lecturer: In the 20th century came the quartz clock. Quartz crystals vibrate at a very precise rate when electricity passes through them.

[2:05] Lecturer: Then, in 1955, the first practical atomic clock was built, using cesium atoms, which vibrate at an extraordinarily constant frequency.

[2:14] Lecturer: Today, atomic clocks carried by satellites are essential for GPS, and precise time also underpins the internet, mobile networks, and banking.

[2:21] Lecturer: That brings us to the end of today's lecture. Next week, we'll look at proposals to reform how we measure the length of a second itself.
